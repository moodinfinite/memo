# Stop Memo's placement test inflating levels

Memo's level check placed a menu-level reader at HSK 5 mainly because **the AI-written "HSK 4–5" items were far easier than their labels, probably by about two levels**. The scoring then turned that error into a promotion. Five items per stage, a "highest level passed" rule and retakes that start at the last result each push the estimate upward, and none of them ever pull it back down. Guessing alone is not the explanation. With honestly labelled items, the chance that a true HSK 2 reader climbs to HSK 5 is about 0.1%. With items two levels too easy and test-wise guessing, it rises to about 60%. The app's labels make things worse. "Read news and magazines" is HSK 2.0 promotional wording that European Chinese teachers reject. They put old HSK 5 at roughly CEFR B1 (lower intermediate). Comfortable newspaper reading takes something like 8,000–11,000 words, not 2,500. The fix needs no psychometrics team. Pick target words in code from official Traditional-character word lists and let Claude only write around them. Replace the staircase with one running estimate that uses every answer and allows for guessing, starting low each time. Add recall formats that cannot be guessed. Cross-check the result against the learner's own flashcard data and one real Taiwanese text. Relabel the levels honestly. The first three steps take days, not weeks.

## Guessing alone cannot reach HSK 5, but easy items plus guessing can

Start with what a 4-option item can and cannot tell you. Psychometricians model multiple-choice answers with a "guessing floor", the lower asymptote c in the three-parameter (3PL) item response model. It is about **0.25 for four options**: even someone who knows nothing gets a quarter right ([ASC](https://assess.com/irt-item-pseudo-guessing-parameter/); [de Ayala, Guilford](https://www.cms.guilford.com/excerpts/deayala_ch6.pdf?t=1)). After correcting for that floor, **a 3/5 "pass" means the learner truly knew only about 47% of the level**, and a 4/5 "climb" means about 73%. Real guessing is also better than random. Learners usually know enough to eliminate a distractor or two, which is why correction formulas that assume blind guessing are considered unrealistic ([Computación y Sistemas](https://cys.cic.ipn.mx/ojs/CyS/article/view/5297)). The documented cues include absurd options and options that echo the stem ([Alberta Journal of Educational Research](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/ajer/article/view/55148/42199)). For "pick the English meaning" items, a Chinese reader can also use radicals or one familiar character in a compound. A realistic effective guess rate for Memo's items is therefore **33–50%, not 25%**.

Even so, the research team's exact calculation over every possible score path shows that guessing alone causes about a one-level error, not three. Under an assumed learning curve, a true HSK 2 learner with honest items and blind guessing ends at HSK 3 or higher 45% of the time but reaches HSK 5 only 0.1% of the time. The first-run path to HSK 5 is narrow. Starting at HSK 2 with four stages, the learner must score at least 4/5 at HSK 2, 3 and 4 in a row and then at least 3/5 at HSK 5. That path only becomes likely when the items behave like lower levels than their labels:

| Items behave like | Effective guess rate | P(result ≥ HSK 4) | P(result ≥ HSK 5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Their label | 25% | 2% | 0.1% |
| Their label | 50% | 22% | 5% |
| 1 level easier | 50% | 62% | 21% |
| 2 levels easier | 25% | 83% | 29% |
| 2 levels easier | 50% | 93% | 61% |

*True HSK 2 learner, first run starting at HSK 2. These are model calculations from the research notes, not measured data.*

The evidence that LLM items run easy is indirect, because no study has compared LLM-written HSK items with official ones, but it is consistent. LLMs steer the *text* of a passage toward a level reasonably well and are poor at hitting a target *item* difficulty. In a May 2026 study, **all LLMs struggled to label hard items, and GPT-5.4 tended to underestimate difficulty**. The authors warn that ever-stronger models will judge most items as easy ([Wang et al., arXiv 2607.28634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28634)). Across 42 LLMs and 793 Cambridge reading items, models could not predict how well items separate stronger from weaker learners. The best correlation was 0.15, and "answer as a weak learner" persona prompts were unreliable ([Chen et al., arXiv 2606.18709](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18709v1)). Teachers rate LLM-written distractors as less plausible than human ones ([arXiv 2404.02124](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.02124)), and LLM distractors tend to be "on-topic but easy to rule out" ([arXiv 2307.16338](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.16338)). For Chinese specifically, direct prompting toward HSK 3.0 levels gives inconsistent over- and under-simplification ([LLM4CGDS, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0952197626001867)). Studies that achieved control did it by constraining the model to level vocabulary lists ([arXiv 2606.21981](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21981)).

Memo's own prompt invites the problem. `api/level-test.ts` asks for distractors that are "clearly wrong to someone who knows the material", lets vocabulary items show a Chinese word and ask for its English meaning, and asks HSK 1–3 reading questions in English. An English question lets a learner match one keyword in the passage without reading the rest. The blind second Claude pass checks the wrong property. It confirms that a fluent reader can answer the item. It cannot tell whether the item is hard enough, and its "does this fit the level?" judgment comes from the same model family that underestimates difficulty. Finally, the format measures **recognition** (picking from options), while reading a menu requires **recall** (producing the meaning with no options) and segmenting unspaced text. Vocabulary-testing researchers name meaning-recognition multiple choice as a source of overestimation and recommend meaning-recall formats ([Stoeckel, McLean & Nation 2021, SSLA](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/limitations-of-size-and-levels-tests-of-written-receptive-vocabulary-knowledge/00A2861F34D3AE984AB2D61AF50F482F); [Gyllstad et al. 2015](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/32284)). In a study of 100 adult L2 Chinese learners, word segmentation predicted reading fluency and comprehension beyond the ability to recognise words in isolation ([Reading and Writing, 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-020-10113-6)). One caveat cuts the other way: recognition scores still *rank* learners well against reading ability ([Cambridge SSLA commentary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/what-the-research-shows-about-written-receptive-vocabulary-testing/6AD9545F1222519998577D96F265BE83)). Multiple choice is not useless. It inflates the absolute level.

## Five items and a "best stage" rule turn noise into promotions

Five items are too few to separate anything. The 95% margin of error on a 5-item score near 60% is **about ±43 percentage points**, so a single stage cannot tell a 30% learner from a 70% one. A learner who truly scores 45%, and should fail a 60% cut, passes a 5-item stage 41% of the time. With 20 items that falls to 13%, and with 40 items to 4%. Each stage also splits its five items across three skills, so one lucky reading item moves the stage by 20 points. Duolingo's adaptive test stopped at 25 items in 2020 and now collects over 200 item measurements per sitting ([Settles et al. 2020, TACL](https://aclanthology.org/2020.tacl-1.17.pdf); [DET Technical Manual 2023](https://www.peb.ufrj.br/images/documentos/DET-technical_manual-V01.pdf)). In item response theory (IRT, the standard statistical model of how ability and item difficulty produce right and wrong answers), a perfectly targeted 4-option item carries limited information. The research team's estimate is **about 26 such items for a standard error of half a level**, against about one full level with 5 items per level.

The scoring rule then adds bias on top of the noise. "Highest level passed at ≥3/5" takes a maximum over noisy results, and a maximum of noisy numbers is systematically higher than the true value. One lucky stage sets the result, and later failures cannot lower it. A run that scores HSK 3 = 2/5, then HSK 2 = 4/5, then HSK 3 = 3/5 is reported as HSK 3, even though the pooled HSK 3 evidence is 5/10, or about a third known after correcting for guessing. The rules are also asymmetric: climbing takes 4/5 but banking a level takes only 3/5, and the stop-at-3/5 rule locks in a borderline pass on five items. Adaptive tests built for classification avoid this. Their stopping rules weigh **all** responses against the evidence at a cut point, not the best segment ([Thompson 2010, ASC](https://assess.com/docs/Thompson_(2010)_-_Advanced_Pass-Fail_Decisions.pdf)).

Starting retakes at the previous result makes the error stick. Modelling five monthly retakes for a true HSK 2 learner with items one level easy, the chance of showing HSK 4 or higher rises from 30% on the first take to 56% by the fifth (blind guessing), or from 40% to 71% (one distractor eliminated). A high start also changes what is reachable. Four stages from HSK 4 can reach HSK 6, which is impossible on a first run. Duolingo does the opposite: it opens every test with deliberately easy items and only then adapts ([Settles et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.tacl-1.17.pdf)).

The writing nudge, (3 × quiz + writing) / 4, does not fix any of this. It blends a separate skill into a reading level and is graded by the same model family. The notes contain no direct evidence on it. Our view is that writing should be reported as its own skill rather than averaged into the placement.

## The "reads news" label was wrong before the test ran

The level chart in `src/lib/levelTest.ts` uses **HSK 2.0 counts** (150 / 300 / 600 / 1,200 / 2,500 / 5,000+ words) and the official HSK 2.0 description of HSK 5: "read news and magazines, follow films". Confucius Institutes publish that description alongside a CEFR C1 label ([Univ. of Valencia Confucius Institute](https://www.uv.es/uvweb/confucius-institute-uv/en/official-examinations/hsk-exams/hsk-exams-1285924056077.html)). The German Chinese teachers' association, Fachverband Chinesisch (FaCh), said in 2010 that it considered it its duty to "categorically deny" that linking. Its reasoning was that every language needs roughly 500 / 1,000 / 2,000 lexical units for A1 / A2 / B1, and HSK assumes about a third of that. FaCh's own mapping is **HSK 3 ≈ A1, HSK 4 ≈ A2, HSK 5 ≈ B1, HSK 6 ≈ B2** ([FaCh 2010](https://www.fachverband-chinesisch.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Der_Verband/Offizielle_Statements/FaCh_2010_HSK-Erklaerung_en.pdf)). It also notes that 2,500 lexical units need only about 1,200 characters. French and Italian estimates are similar, and a German university teacher-training regulation sets HSK 6 = B2 at the end of eight semesters ([Wikipedia: HSK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanyu_Shuiping_Kaoshi); [FAU FPO 2023](https://www.doc.zuv.fau.de/L1/PO/Lehramt/Chinesisch/konsolidierte_Fassungen/LA_Erweiterungsfach_Chinesisch_FPO_LA_Chin._20230822.pdf)). On that reading, even a correctly measured HSK 5 is a B1 learner who handles familiar everyday texts with support.

The scale is also out of date. **HSK 3.0 launches worldwide on 13 December 2026** ([Shanghai Municipal Education Commission](https://edu.sh.gov.cn/study_en_news/20260915/f7034aa15cad4a92a6183abeea104895.html)) and becomes the standard version from January 2027 ([Khanji School](https://khanjischool.com/blog/chinese/hsk-3-0-launch-date-2026)). Its November 2025 exam syllabus sets HSK 3–6 at 1,000 / 2,000 / 3,600 / 5,400 words and HSK 7–9 at 11,000 ([Wikipedia: HSK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanyu_Shuiping_Kaoshi)). An "HSK 5" learner with 2,500 words in the app's sense is roughly **new HSK 4**. The two published "HSK 3.0" lists (the 2021 standard and the exam syllabus) also disagree on 41.5% of shared vocabulary ([harukicoder/hsk30](https://github.com/harukicoder/hsk30)), so any label must name its version.

For a Traditional-script reader who uses Taiwanese Chinese, Taiwan's TOCFL is the more natural yardstick. Its suggested vocabulary sizes, **500 / 1,000 / 2,500 / 5,000 / 8,000 words for CEFR A1 to C1** ([University of Zurich](https://aoi.uzh.ch/en/sinologie/fremdsprache/top.html)), sit close to FaCh's cross-language figures. Its reference framework (TBCL) is built on Taiwan usage ([Wikipedia: TOCFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_of_Chinese_as_a_Foreign_Language)). HSK lists are Simplified and Mainland-based, so asking Claude for "an HSK 5 item in Traditional" adds one more uncontrolled step.

The coverage arithmetic shows why newspapers sit so far up. Characters are deceptive: the top 1,000 cover about 90% of running text and the top 2,000 about 98% ([Wikipedia: character frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_character_frequency)). That is why an old-HSK-5 reader, with 1,685 characters, recognises most characters on a news page. Words grow far more slowly: about 4,900 words cover 80% of a general corpus and about 14,000 cover 90% (same source). Unassisted comprehension needs roughly **98% of running words known**, with 95% as a minimum ([Hu & Nation, via Applied Linguistics 2024](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article/45/6/953/7841943)). Expository text such as news may need close to 100% ([System 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1475158522000741)). These thresholds come from English research, and no one has measured HSK-list coverage of a Chinese newspaper. On the available evidence, comfortable news reading sits around TOCFL 5 / HSK 3.0 levels 7–9, roughly 8,000–11,000 words and 3,000 characters.

Menus are a separate problem. They draw on food-only characters for cooking methods and ingredients, use four-character dish names with no context, and are often poetic or handwritten. A long-term Taiwan resident who had learned thousands of characters reports still meeting unknown characters on menus ([Hacking Chinese](https://www.hackingchinese.com/6-things-in-chinese-that-are-harder-to-learn-than-they-seem/); [Sinosplice](https://www.sinosplice.com/life/archives/2007/11/29/how-to-learn-to-order-food-in-chinese)). This evidence is anecdotal. Struggling with menus is normal up to the intermediate range, so the menu is a poor yardstick in both directions. Even so, "barely reads a menu" is incompatible with a C1 "reads news" claim.

The table below is a suggested set of honest labels. It is our synthesis from FaCh, the TOCFL tables and the coverage figures, not official wording.

| App level (HSK 2.0 words) | ≈ HSK 3.0 (2025 syllabus) | ≈ TOCFL | ≈ CEFR (FaCh) | Honest can-do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (150) | below level 1 | Novice | pre-A1 | Greetings, numbers, a handful of characters |
| 2 (300) | level 1 | Novice | A1.1 | Set phrases; recognises a few dozen characters |
| 3 (600) | level 2–3 | Level 1 | A1 | Survival phrases; very simple signs; not menus |
| 4 (1,200) | level 3 | Level 2 | A2 | Short simple texts on familiar topics; spots dishes by category characters |
| 5 (2,500) | level 4 | Level 3 | B1 | Graded readers and everyday notices with a dictionary; news is a slog; menus partly readable |
| 6 (5,000+) | level 5–6 | Level 4 | B2 | Gist of news articles with lookups |
| beyond the app | levels 7–9 | Level 5 | C1 | Newspapers with occasional lookups |

## Proven short tests take difficulty from outside the item writer

The best-documented short test, Duolingo's DET, rests on one principle: **never trust the writer's difficulty label; compute it from an external standard**. DET put word difficulty on a scale anchored to CEFR-labelled word lists and scored learners with a Rasch model, the simplest IRT model, in which the chance of a correct answer depends only on ability minus item difficulty. This needed no pilot testing. A later fit of real response data from 21,351 sessions ranked test takers almost identically (**Spearman ρ = .96**). Internal consistency was .96 and test-retest .80 ([Settles et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.tacl-1.17.pdf)). DET opens with **15–18 adaptive yes/no vocabulary items, about half real words and half plausible pseudowords**, and reports an EAP score: the average of the posterior, which is the probability distribution over ability after all answers ([DET scoring overview 2024](https://duolingo-papers.s3.amazonaws.com/reports/Duolingo_whitepaper_test_scoring_2024_v1.pdf)). Yes/no items are scored with a signal-detection measure that subtracts false "yes" answers to pseudowords, so saying yes to everything earns nothing. DET still had humans review every passage and pseudoword. Memo's all-LLM pipeline skips that step, which is why deterministic checks matter.

Chinese has a direct analogue. **LexCHI** is a 60-item yes/no test of two-character words, 40 real and 20 nonwords, where each nonword combines two real characters that do not form a word. Reliability was α = .96, and it correlated r = .81 with a cloze test in a first L2 sample but only .47 in a lower-proficiency sample ([Wen et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10991040/)). Two lessons follow. The format is fast (about 3–5 minutes) and reliable, but a fixed list tuned for advanced users discriminates poorly among beginners, so items should be sampled by level band and presented adaptively. LexCHI is also Simplified only, and its authors caution against using it with Traditional-script readers, so Memo would need its own Traditional version. Character-recognition tests sampled by frequency band are similarly reliable (α ≈ .97) and correlate about .5 with HSK level. Asking for the *meaning* gave slightly better test quality than asking for pinyin ([Zhang, Kim & Zhang 2022, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8990832/)). Word-level items predicted reading better than character-level ones (.81 vs .68 against cloze in LexCHI's first study).

On format, recall beats recognition. Meaning recall added significant prediction of reading proficiency beyond meaning recognition, but recognition added nothing beyond recall ([Stewart et al. 2023, Language Testing](https://ma.x-mol.com/paper/1650614257289277440)). Short-answer formats greatly reduce guessing compared with multiple choice and yes/no ([Baradaran et al. 2009](https://www.ensani.ir/fa/article/download/618119)). An "I don't know" option cuts guessing but also hides partial knowledge ([MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/21412-the-i-don-t-know-option-in-the-vocabulary-size-test)), so it suits multiple choice that is kept, not recall items. C-tests are passages with parts of words deleted for the learner to restore. They are a core DET task and an established quick proficiency measure, but Chinese versions are still unvalidated ([DET Technical Manual](https://www.peb.ufrj.br/images/documentos/DET-technical_manual-V01.pdf); [Georgetown AELRC](https://aelrc.georgetown.edu/research/current-projects/c-tests/)).

On length and stopping, adaptive tests usually stop when the standard error of the ability estimate drops below about 0.30–0.35 logits (the log-odds units IRT uses), with minimum and maximum item counts. Fixed lengths of 8 items or fewer noticeably raised the share of unreliable results ([JMIR 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11951945/); [Behavior Research Methods 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-018-1068-x)). Taiwan's own TOCFL adaptive test saves **more than 30% of items** at equal precision, and it does not pre-select a band ([EdUHK](https://repository.eduhk.hk/en/publications/the-development-and-efficiency-of-the-computerized-adaptive-testi/); [Taiwan MOE](https://english.moe.gov.tw/cp-117-34006-5e801-1.html)). For a single yes/no decision such as "is this learner at least level 3?", the sequential probability ratio test (SPRT), which keeps testing until the evidence clearly favours one side of the cut, needs fewer items than a sequential Bayes procedure ([Spray & Reckase 1996](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/jedbes/v21y1996i4p405-414.html)).

Self-assessment is a useful prior and a poor substitute. It correlates about r ≈ .45 with test scores across 67 studies. The correlation is higher for concrete real-life can-do tasks, and researchers recommend using it alongside testing, not instead of it ([Winke et al. 2023, SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/786D4E0E0D500DCDD43CCE24ED610592/core-reader)). Novice learners tend to overestimate themselves ([BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/7871)). That makes this learner's own "can barely read a menu" more striking: it is an underclaim that the test overruled.

No consumer Chinese app has published validity data for its placement test ([HSKLord](https://hsklord.com/hsk-placement-test); [Mandarin Temple](https://beta.mandarintemple.com/guide/getting-started/find-your-level)), so Memo has no industry standard to fall short of. A 10–20 minute test built on these principles could plausibly correlate about .7–.8 with a full exam and land within one band 85–95% of the time. That is the research team's estimate, extrapolated from DET and LexCHI, and it would need checking against learners with real TOCFL or HSK results.

## A prioritized rebuild for one developer

The order below puts the cheapest, highest-impact changes first. Tier 1 alone would have prevented the HSK 5 result.

| Priority | Change | Effort | Fixes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Restart every test at HSK 1–2; use the last result only as a weak prior | Hours | Retake ratchet |
| 1 | Replace "highest level passed" with a pooled estimate that allows for guessing; 15–40 items | 1–2 days | Max bias, 5-item noise, 3/5 = 47% |
| 1 | Rewrite level labels with version, TOCFL/CEFR equivalents and honest can-dos | Hours | Misleading "reads news" |
| 2 | Code picks target words, grammar points and distractors from word lists; Claude only writes the sentence | 2–4 days | Items easier than labelled |
| 2 | Automatic level check of every generated item, plus a choices-only check | 1 day | Out-of-level words, giveaway options |
| 3 | Yes/no word block with pseudowords; typed meaning and pinyin recall | 2–3 days | Guessing, recognition vs recall |
| 3 | Flashcard-data prior and a cross-check flag | 1 day | Undetected large errors |
| 4 | Post-test check on a real Traditional text; timed reading | 1–2 days | Recognition without connected reading |

**Tier 1: scoring and labels.** First, change the start rule in `src/lib/levelTest.ts` so every run opens at HSK 1–2, as DET does, and feeds the previous result in only as a broad prior. Second, replace the staircase arithmetic with a small Bayesian estimator, roughly 100 lines of TypeScript and no calibration data. Keep a grid of possible abilities from 0.5 to 6.5 in steps of 0.05, with a broad prior. Model each answer as P(correct) = c + (1 − c) / (1 + e^(−a(θ − δ))), where θ (theta) is the learner's ability on the HSK scale, δ (delta) is the item's level taken from the word list, c is the guessing floor (1/number of options for multiple choice, 0 for typed answers), and a is a slope, set to about 1.5–2 per level as an untested starting guess. After each answer, multiply the grid by the answer's likelihood. Choose the next item at the boundary between the two most likely levels. Stop at a minimum of 15 and a maximum of about 40 items, or earlier once one level holds 80% of the probability. Report conservatively: the highest level L where P(θ ≥ L) is at least 0.7, shown as a range ("HSK 2–3, most likely 2"). Store the full distribution, not just the integer. Partial-credit answers fit the same formula as a soft grade between 0 and 1, as in DET. If this is too much at once, an interim patch is to pool all answers at each level, correct for guessing, and require at least 10 items at a level before calling it passed. Third, rewrite the `HSK_LEVELS` descriptions using the table above. Name the scale version, drop "read news and magazines", and put a CEFR and TOCFL equivalent next to each level. For this learner, consider making TOCFL bands the primary scale.

**Tier 2: anchored items.** Import a machine-readable list with Traditional forms. `ivankra/hsk30` has 11,092 HSK 3.0 terms with the Taiwan-common Traditional form listed first, plus grammar-point and character lists ([GitHub](https://github.com/ivankra/hsk30)). `ivankra/tocfl` has Taiwan's own 7,517-term list ([GitHub](https://www.github.com/ivankra/tocfl)). `drkameleon/complete-hsk-vocabulary` carries both HSK 2.0 and 3.0 levels, frequency rank, part of speech and CC-CEDICT meanings ([GitHub](https://github.com/drkameleon/complete-hsk-vocabulary)). Both HSK repos are MIT-licensed code built on government lists whose copyright status is ambiguous, so check the licences before redistributing. Once a list is in place, the code chooses the target word or grammar point at level L, and also chooses distractors from the same level and part of speech, ideally sharing a character with the target. Claude is asked only to write a Traditional carrier sentence using no word above L. The prompt's "clearly wrong" instruction should go. Ask comprehension questions in Chinese from HSK 2 upward. After generation, segment each item, look up every word's level, and reject the item if any non-name word sits above L, or if a passage has more than about 2–5% out-of-level words. That threshold is a suggestion, not a researched figure. Finally, replace the blind "do you agree with the key" pass with a **choices-only check**: show Claude only the options, and drop any item it still answers correctly well above chance. That kind of answerable-without-the-question shortcut has been documented in benchmarks with model-written distractors ([arXiv 2402.12483](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12483v1)). A finer difficulty signal comes free with the list: a word's frequency rank within its level can serve as a continuous δ.

**Tier 3: formats and the app's own data.** Add a yes/no block of 20–30 two-character words per band, with about a third pseudowords. Build the pseudowords in code by pairing real same-level characters and discarding any pair that appears in CC-CEDICT or the word lists. Don't have Claude invent them, since it may produce real rare words. Score the block with LexCHI's normalised Ghent score, (yes to real words − 2 × yes to pseudowords) / number of real words ([Wen et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10991040/)). Then ask for typed English meanings for a subset of the "yes" words, graded by fuzzy match against the dictionary glosses, with Claude judging near-misses and giving soft grades of 0, 0.5 or 1. Add tone-tolerant pinyin typing, which needs no LLM at all. Keep multiple choice only for grammar and reading, with 5–6 options or an explicit 不知道 (I don't know) button. Next, use the flashcard and SRS (spaced-repetition) history. For each level, compute the share of list words the learner has stably mastered. The highest level where cumulative coverage reaches about 80% makes a sensible prior centre. This is a heuristic, because no published threshold exists. Draw about 70% of test words from words the learner has **not** studied, so the test measures transfer rather than flashcard memory, and report studied-word accuracy separately as retention. If the test estimate lands more than one level above the SRS estimate, show both, mark the result "uncertain", and don't save the higher one.

**Tier 4: a reality check before announcing a level.** Show one short real-world Traditional text at the estimated level and one level below: a menu at the low end, a notice or MRT sign in the middle, a news paragraph higher up. Ask "how much did you understand?" plus one or two typed questions graded by Claude. Cap the reported level at the highest text the learner both rated as at least 75% understood and answered correctly. Human-written or authentic texts keep the LLM's easy bias out of the check. Time the reading items: the official HSK 5 reading section allows about a minute per question ([chinesetest.cn](https://www.chinesetest.cn/HSK/5?type=2)), while an untimed item lets a slow character-by-character decoder pass. Log response times as a secondary signal. Over time, store every item and answer. Elo-style ratings, which update learner and item together after each answer like a chess match, recover item difficulty reasonably well from few responses (r = .70 at 5 responses per item, .91 at 50; [Pankiewicz, ICCE 2020](https://library.apsce.net/index.php/ICCE/article/view/3939)). A single-user app will accumulate this slowly, so treat it as confirmation, not as the primary anchor.

Some things remain unknown. No study has measured how much easier LLM-written HSK or TOCFL items are than official ones. The slope and level-spacing values in the estimator are assumptions. The staircase probabilities come from a modelled learner, not real data. The cheapest way to settle all three is to have a few learners with real TOCFL or HSK certificates take the rebuilt test.

## Conclusion

The HSK 5 result shows that an LLM can write an item but cannot be trusted to say how hard it is. The current pipeline asks Claude for both and then asks Claude again to confirm, so every check shares the same blind spot. The durable fix is to move difficulty out of the model and into data the developer controls: official word lists for item levels, a likelihood model for scoring, and the learner's own flashcard history and real Taiwanese texts for sanity checks. Claude then does what it is good at, writing natural sentences and grading free-text answers, inside guardrails it cannot talk its way past.

The labels matter as much as the arithmetic. Even a perfectly measured "HSK 5" would have told this learner they could read the news when, by teachers' and coverage evidence, they are a lower-intermediate reader. Showing a range on an honest, versioned scale (TOCFL or HSK 3.0 with CEFR equivalents) costs nothing and turns the level check from a flattering number into something the learner can plan around. That matters more with HSK 3.0 replacing the scale the app was built on in December 2026.
